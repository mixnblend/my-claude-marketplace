# ASD-STE100 Writing Rules — Section Summary

This is a working summary of the rule categories in ASD-STE100, written to guide general technical documentation. It is not a reproduction of the standard.

## Citing the standard

The standard is published by ASD, the AeroSpace and Defence Industries Association of Europe, and is a free download from <https://www.asd-ste100.org/>. New issues appear every few years, and both the exact rule count and the rule numbering change between issues.

Do not cite a specific rule number, issue number, issue date, or dictionary word count from this file. Check the current issue at the URL above before you put any of those in a document. This file is deliberately written without them.

## Part 1 — Writing Rules

The writing rules are organised into nine sections.

### 1. Words

Use only approved words from the dictionary, plus the technical names and technical verbs your project needs. Each approved word carries one meaning and one part of speech. Where a word is approved as a noun, do not use it as a verb.

Keep the same word for the same idea throughout a document. Synonym variation reads as a signal to the reader that something changed. Choose short, common words over long or formal ones.

Applied generally: pick the plainest word available, then use it consistently. Do not alternate between *check*, *verify*, *validate*, and *confirm* for one action.

### 2. Noun Phrases

Limit a noun cluster to three words. Break longer clusters apart with prepositions or a relative clause. Long clusters are ambiguous because the reader cannot tell which noun modifies which.

*high pressure fuel pump inlet valve* → *the inlet valve on the high-pressure fuel pump*

### 3. Verbs

Use only the approved form of each verb. Use simple present, simple past, and simple future. Avoid the perfect tenses, the continuous tenses, and compound constructions.

Use the active voice in procedures. In descriptive text, use the active voice unless the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.

Do not use a verb as a noun, and do not use a noun as a verb.

### 4. Sentences

Keep procedural sentences to 20 words or fewer, and descriptive sentences to 25 words or fewer. Keep paragraphs to six sentences or fewer in descriptive text.

Write one topic per paragraph. Do not omit words that make the structure explicit — keep articles, relative pronouns, and the subject of each clause. Omitting them saves space but creates ambiguity.

### 5. Procedural Writing

Give one instruction per sentence. Where a sentence contains two actions, split it.

Start with the imperative verb. State the condition or the purpose before the action, so the reader reads the constraint before acting on the instruction.

Use a numbered list for an ordered sequence, and a bulleted list for an unordered set.

### 6. Descriptive Writing

Descriptive text permits slightly longer sentences than procedures, but the same rules on voice, tense, and word choice apply.

Split a long description into short paragraphs, each with one topic. Use a table where the content has consistent fields across entries.

### 7. Safety Instructions

Place a warning or caution immediately before the step it applies to, never after it and never collected at the end.

Start a warning with a clear command, and state the consequence of ignoring it. Keep the condition first: *Before you open the panel, isolate the supply.*

Applied generally: this covers destructive commands, irreversible migrations, and anything that touches production.

### 8. Punctuation and Word Counts

Use simple punctuation. Avoid the slash as a substitute for *and* or *or*, because the reader cannot tell which one is meant. Avoid parentheses that carry load-bearing meaning; if the content matters, put it in its own sentence.

Word counts are the sentence and paragraph limits given in section 4.

### 9. Writing Practices

Be consistent across the whole document. Define each project-specific term once, in a glossary, and then use it unchanged.

Prefer a positive statement over a negative one. Prefer two short sentences over one long sentence joined by a conjunction.

## Part 2 — Dictionary

The dictionary lists approved words with a single meaning and a single part of speech, and it lists words to avoid together with an approved replacement. Alongside it, a project may define its own technical names and technical verbs where no approved word carries the meaning.

Technical names are the nouns your domain requires — *idempotency key*, *replica lag*, *circuit breaker*. Technical verbs are the actions your domain requires — *deploy*, *serialise*, *rebase*.

This skill does not reproduce the dictionary. It applies the principle instead: plainest word available, used the same way every time, with domain terms kept and defined once.
